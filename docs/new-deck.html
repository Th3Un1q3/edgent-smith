Observations & Risks Found

What slows AI adoption at [Company] down

- I'd like to list my findings from talks with [Employees], - These driven ideas to follow.

PR Review Noise

What it looks like Each PR collects peer review, SonarCloud audit, non-actionable AWS security-agent feedback, lint, and tests
How it's proven Alerts and bot comments per PR keep climbing; change lead time grows while reviewers approve unread and click resolve to unblock merges
Why it matters Integration slows down; resolving becomes the path of least resistance, so compliance checks stop meaning anything
This finding shares a root with the next one: too much low-trust automation at the PR stage. This slide covers the input side. Each pull request collects peer review, SonarCloud, the security agent, lint, and tests. That is too many channels for one person to triage. The measurements back it up. Alerts and bot comments per PR keep climbing. Change lead time grows while reviewers approve without reading and click resolve just to unblock merges. When resolving is the path of least resistance, automated compliance stops meaning anything. Lead time sits on this slide deliberately, not under Slop. Slop is about what changes contain. This is about how many signals bury them.

Slop

What it looks like PRs and task requirements carry slop: low-quality AI-generated content, growing volume, growing size
How it's proven Average PR size trends above baseline; comment counts land at extremes, near-zero or flooded; eNPS drifts down as cleanup piles up
Why it matters Output quality declines; fighting slop daily feeds resistance to AI adoption
Slop is the output half of the same root. The problem here is the content of the changes themselves: low-quality AI output and inflated change size. Everyone I spoke with complained about slop, and that matches my own experience. Default setups produce it unless someone tunes them for quality. My evidence is partly anecdotal, so the slide leans on three checkable measures. Average PR size runs above baseline. Comment counts land at extremes, either silent approval or flooding. eNPS drifts down because people who constantly clean up slop enjoy their work less. Together that breeds resistance to AI adoption: people who fight slop every day conclude that AI makes things worse.

No Agent Portability & Isolation

What it looks like Agents run only where someone manually configured them, on individual developer machines; every agent needs hands-on setup
How it's proven Environments take days to build, reproduce, install, and configure; no two developers' setups match; zero agent runs outside laptops
Why it matters Agents cannot scale beyond developer machines; unguarded machines risk runaway agents
Every agent exists only where someone manually configured it, which means one developer's machine. Nothing reproduces, so nothing moves. No cloud run, no shared runner, no reuse. The proof is the environment itself. Days to build, impossible to duplicate exactly, a different snowflake on every laptop. Agents therefore cannot scale beyond developer machines, and an unguarded machine invites runaway agents doing unintended work. Dev containers and dev pods fix this by making the environment reproducible; they come later in the Ideas chapter.

Silos

What it looks like Teams sit at visibly different adoption stages; each picks its own pace and tooling, and nobody shares a bar for progress
How it's proven Teams rebuild the same tools, MCP configs, and skills across the org
Why it matters Nobody builds the paved road; effort is duplicated, and lessons do not travel between teams
Every team still figures out its tooling on its own, even though the push to adopt AI is strong. Look across the org and the teams sit at visibly different stages. Different MCPs, different skills, different setups. The duplication doubles as the measurement: teams solve the same problems again and again. Nobody builds a paved road that way. Effort goes to waste, and lessons stay trapped inside team boundaries, which drives individual frustration.

One-Off Agent Environments

What it looks like Agent environments are one-offs — they do not represent a system
How it's proven Everyone configures own agentic env
Why it matters Hard to maintain, learn from mistakes, evolve, audit and fix
Most of the agentic environments are not portable. They exist only on a developer's machine and vary from developer to developer. That stops us from learning from mistakes and limits how the system can evolve. We are paying for that as a company, not just on individual desks.

Missing Unified Roadmap

What it looks like No roadmap tells teams the next step, how far they have come, or whether they move the right way
How it's proven Adoption activity spikes during workshops and dies right after; people switch to passive mode, and skills appear only when a workshop forces them
Why it matters Effort does not compound; momentum dies between events, so today's PR and quality bottlenecks persist
Workshops on things like spec-driven development are working, and credit goes to the people running them. Look at the pattern, though. Progress shows up during the event. Once it ends, people switch to passive mode. Skills appear only when a workshop forces them, and contribution stops between events. Without a roadmap nobody can tell how far adoption has come or whether it moves in the right direction. Teams have direction without a sequence. So effort does not compound. Harness engineering and infrastructure stay under-prioritized, and the PR and quality bottlenecks persist.

Decisions Without Knowledge

What it looks like Institutional knowledge doesn't power better decisions — agents can't validate plans against the architecture or judge architectural decisions
How it's proven ADRs exist but aren't integrated with agents
Why it matters Plans drift from the architecture; architectural decisions go unjudged
Agents still depend on institutional knowledge that is provided manually. They cannot be powered by the ADRs, so they cannot spot conflicts between ADRs, and they cannot look back at post-mortems to avoid repeating the same mistake. The result is that feedback loops and knowledge are not being captured, preserved, and made available to the agents. Every agent run then starts from a weaker position than it should.

Cross-Domain Ownership Blur

What it looks like AI lowers the bar to jump between stacks — frontend, backend, Jenkins, and infra land in the same PRs, and no one owns the boundaries
How it's proven Mixed PRs with cross-stack changes; confusion about who owns major codebase areas
Why it matters Blurred responsibility — PRs get merged by people who don't fully understand every part they touch
AI makes it cheap to be productive in a stack you don't own, and it shows. Frontend devs change backend code; backend devs change Jenkins and infra. PRs get bigger, and the person approving doesn't fully understand part of the change. That blurs ownership of the major areas of our codebase and starts arguments about who is responsible. The fix is not to forbid it — it's defaults that make the boundaries visible and permissioned, which is the Governance & Defaults idea in the Ideas chapter.

AI Fatigue & Social Erosion

What it looks like The push to "use AI" is wearing people down — AI-generated ad-hoc text, project stories, and automated replies clutter channels and erode trust
How it's proven Devs report being tired of the AI message; channel signal is drowning in AI-generated noise
Why it matters Noise drowns signal — trust and collaboration norms erode, and the adoption message stops landing
The other side of adoption is the people who are tired of hearing about it. Combined with AI-generated text in channels and automated replies, the message gets loud and the signal gets thin. That erodes the trust and collaboration norms the push depends on. The path forward is a calmer, measurement-first message from middle management — that is what the Middle Management Briefing idea covers. Less hype, more proof.

Technology & Language Inequality

What it looks like AI value is uneven across stacks — big wins in TypeScript and Python; legacy, specialized, and low-level stacks see little of it
How it's proven AI assisted coding receives negative estimate from non-mainstream langs devs; active resistance from older, experienced engineers
Why it matters Uneven adoption — the engineers with the deepest context resist a change AI can't serve
The wins we celebrate are real, but they cluster in TypeScript and Python. In the core network team, working in C, the models don't understand much of the context, and the senior engineers there feel the push ignores their reality. That is where the active resistance comes from. The answer is not to push harder but to build the per-stack skills and context that make AI useful in those domains — the Centralized Skills Repository idea starts there.
